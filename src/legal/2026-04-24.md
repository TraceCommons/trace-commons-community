This is the whole of it: what you agree to, what happens to your data, what each
consent scope permits, and how to take it back.

Each section says the plain thing first, in ordinary words. The exact clause
follows it, indented. Where the two differ the clause is what governs — so if a
plain-language summary here is wrong, that is a fault to be fixed in this
document, not a licence to read the clause loosely.

Every trace submission records the policy version it was made under. These terms
apply to a trace as they read on the day it was submitted.

## B. Data policy

### B.1 What a submission actually contains

A redacted envelope, and nothing else. It carries the conversation events of one
agent session, metadata about the engine and model that produced it, counts of
what redaction removed, and hashes. It does not carry your raw session file,
your filesystem, or your credentials.

Nothing is sent without your approval, but approval is not always
item-by-item: the default is that you see a preview of each trace and approve
it, and there are two ways to approve in advance — arming a project for
automatic upload, and approving a queue in bulk. Both send traces you have not
individually looked at. That is your choice to make, and worth making
deliberately.

> **Clause B.1.** Each submission is a structured envelope comprising: session
> and event records for a single agent session; engine, channel and model
> metadata; consent metadata (the policy version, the granted scopes, and flags
> recording whether message text and tool payloads are included); privacy
> metadata (redaction pipeline version, per-category redaction counts,
> residual-risk label, redaction hash, and any warnings); and a pseudonymous
> contributor reference with a revocation handle. The consent flags are
> descriptive: they record what the payload contains, and are not a setting that
> changes it.
>
> No trace is transmitted without an approval recorded for it. An approval may
> be given for an individual trace after preview, or in advance by arming a
> project for automatic upload or by approving a queue in bulk; in the latter
> two cases the trace is transmitted without having been previewed.

### B.2 Redaction happens on your machine first

Discovery, parsing and deterministic secret scrubbing all run locally, before
anything leaves your machine, and `submit --dry-run` shows you exactly what
would go.

One exception, and it is opt-in: the client has a `--pii-filter near-ai` mode
that sends your text to NEAR AI, under your own API key, as part of cleaning it.
Off unless you turn it on. Separately, the server sends text to NEAR AI after
receipt — see B.3 and B.4.

> **Clause B.2.** The contributor client discovers local agent session files,
> parses them, and applies deterministic redaction locally. Only the redacted
> envelope is transmitted. Where the contributor has enabled the optional
> model-based local filter, text is sent to NEAR AI under the contributor's own
> credentials during redaction.
>
> The server re-applies deterministic redaction on receipt. This is a second
> pass, not a check against the client: where the server removes something the
> client did not, the envelope's redaction counts and hash are updated to the
> server's result, a warning is recorded on the envelope, and the residual-risk
> label may rise. The submission is not rejected for the difference.

### B.3 What happens after it arrives

Deterministic redaction runs again, the trace is encrypted and stored, and its
admission is decided from the resulting risk label. Two further things happen
afterwards rather than at the door: a model-based privacy filter re-examines
message text, and novelty and complexity scoring decides whether the trace
enters the corpus.

While the model filter is enabled — it is, on this deployment — a trace
carrying message text is held, and not admitted, until that filter has run over
it. If the filter keeps failing, the trace ends up quarantined for a human to
look at rather than admitted unexamined.

> **Clause B.3.** On receipt the server re-applies deterministic redaction and
> stores the resulting artifact encrypted under a per-artifact data key, itself
> wrapped by a key held in Google Cloud KMS. Admission at receipt is determined
> by the residual-PII risk label: submissions above the deployment's configured
> tolerance are quarantined. This deployment currently accepts submissions
> labelled medium risk.
>
> Where the asynchronous model-based privacy filter is enabled, a submission
> containing message text is held in a pending state until that filter has
> re-examined and, where necessary, further redacted it; repeated filter failure
> results in quarantine rather than admission. Novelty and perplexity scoring,
> which determines corpus admission, likewise runs asynchronously after receipt.
> Both are deployment-configurable controls, and this document states how this
> deployment is configured rather than guaranteeing a configuration.

### B.4 Who else touches it

Two companies, for two jobs:

- **Google Cloud** — hosting, the database, encrypted object storage and the key
  management service, in the US-Central region.
- **NEAR AI** — the privacy filter and the scoring model both run there, on
  hardware-isolated inference. Message text is sent to it, in chunks, as part of
  processing every submission.

That second one is worth reading twice: content you submit is sent to a third
party as a normal part of processing it. It is redacted before it goes, and it
goes to a trusted-execution environment rather than an ordinary API, but it does
leave our infrastructure.

> **Clause B.4.** We use the following sub-processors: Google LLC (compute,
> managed PostgreSQL, object storage and key management, region us-central1);
> and NEAR AI (TEE-hosted large-language-model inference, used for the prose-PII
> privacy filter and for perplexity scoring). Redacted message text is
> transmitted to the latter as part of processing each submission. We will
> publish changes to this list by amending this document.

### B.5 Our logs do not contain your content

Audit rows and our own error logs record hashes and labels rather than trace
bodies, URLs, tokens or contributor identity. That is how the code is written.
It is a property of what we log deliberately, not a guarantee about every log a
running server can produce: a deployment turned up to a verbose level can record
more, and a component we did not write can log what it was given.

> **Clause B.5.** Stored audit records and the service's own operational log
> statements are hash-only or label-only by design: raw trace content,
> credentials and identifying references are excluded from them. This does not
> extend to diagnostic output produced by third-party libraries at verbose log
> levels, which we control by configuration rather than by design.

### B.6 How long we keep it

Every submission is stamped at receipt with an expiry date, set by the
retention policy for what it is allowed to be used for — two years for most
traces, three for those admitted for model training. Withdrawal removes a trace
before then; nothing else does today, because the pruning that would act on
those dates is not switched on. So in practice a trace persists until you
withdraw it, and it carries a date on which it is due to go.

> **Clause B.6.** Each submission is assigned a retention policy at receipt,
> which fixes an expiry date derived from its permitted uses. Automated pruning
> against those dates is not currently enabled on this deployment; a submission
> therefore persists until withdrawn or until pruning is enabled and reaches it.
> Superseded copies of a stored object are removed by a storage lifecycle rule,
> and deleted copies remain recoverable from the storage provider's soft-delete
> buffer for a further seven days, after which they are purged.

### B.7 What the public figures do and do not protect

Published aggregates drop any breakdown cell holding fewer than two traces.
Read that precisely: it counts traces, not contributors, so a cell of two traces
from one contributor is published, and the headline totals are never suppressed
at all. It is suppression rather than statistical noise — it stops the most
obvious re-identification and is not a guarantee against a determined analyst
combining several published figures. A calibrated noise mechanism is intended as
the contributor base grows; it does not exist yet, and the snapshot records that
it was published on a suppression-only basis.

> **Clause B.7.** Community aggregate breakdowns are published under a
> minimum-cell threshold of two records per cell, with a declared publication
> basis of suppression only. Top-level totals are not subject to that threshold.
> No differential privacy or noise-addition mechanism is currently applied.
> Leaderboard rows are published only for contributors who have granted
> `public_attribution`.

## C. Consent scopes

You pick these at enrolment, and they travel inside every envelope you submit.
They are cumulative in what they permit, except the last, which is about your
name rather than your traces. What a scope does not name, it does not permit.

### C.1 `debugging_evaluation` — the default

Lets us debug the system, evaluate how agents perform, and count your trace in
aggregate figures. Does not permit training anything.

### C.2 `benchmark_only`

Adds permission to build benchmarks from the trace. Still no training.

### C.3 `ranking_training`

Everything above, plus training rankers — models that score or order results.
Not generative model training.

### C.4 `model_training`

Everything above, plus inclusion in training data for models. This is the
broadest trace-content scope, and the one to think hardest about, because a
trained model cannot be untrained — see D.3.

### C.5 `public_attribution` — not about your traces

Links your pseudonymous account to a public handle on the leaderboard and
profile pages. It grants no use of trace content whatsoever, and it is not
required to contribute. An enrolment holding only this scope cannot submit
traces at all.

### C.6 What the scopes bind

> **Clause C.6.** The scopes above correspond exactly to the consent scope
> values recorded in the envelope. A use not permitted by a scope recorded in a
> given trace's envelope is not permitted for that trace, regardless of any
> scope granted later or by another contributor. As stated in Clause A.5, this
> is a limit we undertake to observe; the software records the scopes with the
> trace and filters access on a permitted-use list derived from them by the
> contributor's client.

## D. Withdrawal and revocation

### D.1 How to take a trace back

Run `trace-commons-contributor daemon withdraw <submission-id>`, or
`--all-quarantined` for everything held in quarantine. Do it while you are still
logged in: withdrawal needs the account session, and logging out deletes it.

> **Clause D.1.** You may withdraw any submission at any time while your
> enrolment is active. Withdrawal revokes the licence granted in Clause A.5 for
> that trace, subject to Clause D.3.

### D.2 What that actually deletes

The encrypted artifact and every stored reference to it, including the copies
made at earlier processing stages. If any part of that deletion fails, the
withdrawal fails rather than reporting success.

Some rows about your trace remain, marked revoked, and we would rather name them
than let you assume otherwise: a short derived summary of the trace, the
embedding computed from it, and the scoring and credit records. They stop being
served and stop counting towards anything published. They are not, today,
deleted.

The bytes go in two stages. The copy being served is deleted at once. The
superseded copy underneath it — object storage keeps one — is swept by a
lifecycle rule and then held in the provider's recovery buffer, so the outer
bound on the encrypted bytes disappearing entirely is roughly ten days rather
than instant.

Public figures that included your trace are recomputed, and the site refuses to
serve a snapshot older than fifteen minutes — so a stale leaderboard breaks
visibly instead of quietly continuing to show you.

> **Clause D.2.** On withdrawal we delete the encrypted trace artifact, the
> stored object references, and artifacts held at status-derived storage paths
> from earlier processing stages. Errors in that deletion propagate as failures
> rather than being reported as success. Superseded copies retained by object
> versioning are removed by a storage lifecycle rule and then by the provider's
> soft-delete expiry; the encrypted bytes are therefore fully purged within
> approximately ten days of withdrawal rather than immediately.
>
> Records derived from the trace — including a canonical summary, vector
> embeddings, gate and scoring decisions, and credit records — are marked
> revoked and excluded from all serving, matching and published aggregation.
> They are retained in that revoked state. Credit already attributed is not
> reversed. We will state in a future policy version when derived records are
> deleted rather than revoked.

### D.3 What withdrawal cannot undo

Three things, and we would rather say so plainly than let you discover them: a
statistic that has already been published stays published, a model that has
already been trained stays trained, and the derived records named in D.2 are
revoked rather than erased. Withdrawal is not retroactive over things computed
from your trace before you withdrew it.

> **Clause D.3.** Withdrawal does not require us to retract aggregate figures
> already published, nor to retrain, alter or destroy models or other derived
> artifacts produced before the withdrawal took effect, nor to delete the
> revoked derived records described in Clause D.2. Aggregates recomputed after
> withdrawal will exclude the withdrawn trace.
